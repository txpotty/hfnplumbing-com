    Frequently Asked Questions About Basement Sump Pump Installation in Asheville

    Prices in Asheville, NC usually land between $800 and $2,500. A pedestal unit runs cheaper than a cast iron submersible, and the biggest swing comes from whether we have to excavate a new pit and whether you want a battery backup added to the same basin. Every quote is itemized.

    Flooding can back up municipal sewers and saturate septic drain fields, causing slow drains and backups inside the home. Outages and storms arrive together, so we recommend a battery backup in most basements. The backup starts on its own if the main pump has no power, and it also catches the water if the primary switch sticks or the pump simply cannot keep up with a heavy inflow. It is cheap insurance.

    A primary pump does the everyday work. The secondary, usually battery powered, sits beside it as a backup, and it kicks on if the primary fails, loses power, or gets overwhelmed by more water than it can move during a heavy storm. Each pump gets its own check valve.

    Much of the housing in North Carolina was built after the 1970s, and many of those homes use CPVC, copper or PEX supply lines. Slab homes without a basement rarely need a pump. Basements and below-grade rooms are a different case, since they can take on water fast during heavy storms, especially where the ground sheds rain rather than soaking it up. A pit costs less than a cleanup.

    Our installs carry a written warranty on both parts and labor. The exact term is spelled out in your service agreement, and the pump manufacturer adds its own coverage, which on a quality cast iron pump usually runs three to five years from the date of purchase. We handle the registration.

    A 1/3 or 1/2 horsepower pump handles most residential basements. What decides it is how fast the pit fills and how high the water rises, so we time the refill with a stopwatch and measure from the pit floor to the discharge exit before picking a model. Oversizing causes short cycling.

    For some homeowners, a same-model pump swap is a fair weekend job. Cutting a new pit or adding a backup is not, because those mistakes end in flooding, cracked slabs or electrical hazards, and fixing them costs more than paying for the install up front. Leave those to us.

    Every two to three months, and before and after the wet season. Dump a bucket of water into the pit slowly, then watch the float lift, the pump start, the water drop, and the pump shut off cleanly without humming or rattling. That is the whole test.
