    Under-Sink Grease Trap Maintenance in High Point, NC

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps are rated by flow, usually 10 to 50 gallons per minute, and they hold far less than people expect. In a busy kitchen, that means cleaning every week or two. The service covers grease and solids removal, a baffle scrape, a look at the inlet flow control fitting, and a new lid gasket if the old one is flattened. A bad gasket is why most of these smell.

    Odor Control and Trap Inspections in High Point

    A trap that stinks between pump-outs usually has a flattened lid gasket or a vent problem. We check both on every visit. Gaskets are cheap to swap, but a broken vent can push sewer gas into the kitchen and slow the drain too, so it is worth chasing. Odors are often fixed the same day.

    Large Outdoor Grease Interceptor Cleaning in High Point

    Gravity interceptors in the parking lot often hold 750 to 2,000 gallons across two or three compartments. We pump each compartment on its own.     Grease Line Jetting in High Point

    Grease that slips past a full trap coats the 4-inch line out to the main. The buildup forms rings that are thickest along the bottom. Snaking just bores a small path that closes up in weeks, but a rotating jetter nozzle strips grease off the full wall of the pipe. We run a camera after to confirm.

    Hydro-Jetting for Grease Lines in High Point, NC

    A cable machine pokes a hole through grease. Jetting actually removes it. Our jetters run around 3,000 to 4,000 psi through a spinning nozzle, which scours the whole inside of the pipe and pushes the loosened grease downstream. A camera pass afterward shows whether the line is really clean.

    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in High Point

    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. Large outdoor interceptors can pass $1,000 when they are full and the cap has hardened. Disposal is billed by the gallon at the receiving facility, so the gallons hauled drive most of the cost, and a 1,500-gallon tank fills a truck.

    A small under-sink trap can be hand-cleaned by staff. Disposal is the hard part. Trap waste generally cannot go in a dumpster or down another drain, and an outdoor interceptor needs a vacuum truck and licensed hauler regardless.

    Watch the floor sinks during dish rush. Water that lingers in them is an early warning. Other signs include a rancid smell near the dish station, grease showing up in the cleanout nearest the street, and drains that gurgle after the dish machine empties.

    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. Haulers call it brown grease. Yellow grease, the used fryer oil in your collection bin, is a separate product that gets recycled and should never go into the trap.

    No permit is needed just to have a trap pumped. The records are your responsibility. A new or resized trap is different, usually needing a plumbing permit and sometimes a review by the pretreatment program first.

    Slow sinks come first. Then gurgling floor drains and a sour smell near the dish area. By the time gray water is coming up through a floor sink, the trap is usually well past 25 percent full and grease is already heading down the line.

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps and in-ground gravity interceptors make up most of our work. Automatic grease removal units are on the list too. An AGRU uses a heater and skimmer wheel to move grease into a bin, and the bin, wheel and strainer all need regular cleaning.

    Fryer volume drives the schedule more than seat count. Plenty of busy restaurants pump monthly. A coffee shop with a light menu may go two or three months, provided the depth readings stay below 25 percent.
