    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 6 min read

    The pipes you never see do some of the hardest work in the house. A water service line brings fresh water in from the meter. A sewer lateral carries everything from the drains out to the city main or the septic tank. Both are buried, often several feet down, and both can fail without much warning. These days a repair rarely means digging up the lawn from one end to the other.

    What Damages Buried Pipes in North Carolina

    Tree roots cause more sewer trouble than anything else. Willows and silver maples are the worst offenders, though plenty of ornamental trees go after sewer lines too. A hairline crack or loose joint leaks warm, nutrient-rich vapor, and the roots follow it right in. Inside, they grow into a mat that snags waste and will in time crack the pipe wide open.

    Coastal areas have sandy soil and high water tables, which affect how sewer lines and septic systems perform.

    Soil movement does damage slowly. Expansive clay swells in wet weather and shrinks in dry spells, while rock holds firm and creates pressure points. Over years that cycle shifts joints and cracks rigid pipe, leaving sags, which plumbers call bellies, where waste sits and builds up. Heavy trucks parked over a shallow line and poor bedding during construction add to the problem.

    Pipe material matters as well. In older neighborhoods it is common to find the original clay tile or cast iron sewer still in the ground. Some houses also have galvanized steel water service lines that rust from the inside out. ABS and PVC, standard from the 1970s onward, will not rust, though they still crack and separate at the joints.

    Much of the housing in North Carolina was built after the 1970s, and many of those homes use CPVC, copper or PEX supply lines.

    Warning Signs of Underground Pipe Damage

    Buried pipe problems show up in roundabout ways. Watch for these:

    • Repeat backups in more than one fixture, starting with whatever drain sits lowest.
    • A gurgling toilet when the washing machine drains.
    • Sewer smell in the yard or around a cleanout.
    • A green or soggy strip of lawn that stands out in a dry spell.
    • Sinkholes or depressions along the path of the line.
    • A water meter that keeps moving with every fixture off, pointing to a service line leak.
    • Low water pressure through the whole house.

    Modern Repair Methods

    Diagnosis comes first. On the sewer side, a waterproof camera goes in at the cleanout and records every foot of pipe out to the main. The footage shows roots, cracks, offsets and bellies, and a locator at the camera head marks the depth and location of each one from the surface. For water service lines, pressure testing and acoustic leak detection narrow down where the water is getting out.

    Cured-in-place lining slides a resin-soaked felt liner into the old sewer, inflates it and lets it cure into a new jointless pipe inside the old one. On a 4 inch lateral, the cured liner takes up very little of the inside diameter, and it flows better than rough cast iron did. It seals cracks and root entry points and usually needs only the cleanout for access. Pipe bursting pulls new HDPE through the line while a cone-shaped head breaks the old pipe outward. Bursting suits pipe too broken to line. Spot repair means digging up one section to swap out one bad joint or a single sag. For water service, directional boring runs a new line from meter to house with a small pit at each end.

    Cost comes down to footage and depth. Expect $150 to $400 for the camera inspection itself. Spot sewer repairs often land between $1,500 and $4,500, trenchless lining or bursting of a full residential lateral commonly runs about $4,000 to $12,000, and a new water service line is roughly $1,500 to $5,000. Trenchless is not always cheaper per foot than an excavator, but you are not paying to rebuild the driveway and the pavers afterward.

    When to Call a Professional

    Drains that keep backing up, or a meter showing a leak you cannot find, call for a professional inspection. Underground work often needs permits and utility locates, and careful digging around gas lines.
